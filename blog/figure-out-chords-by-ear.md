To figure out chords by ear, don't try to hear the whole chord at once. Loop one chord and find its lowest note, which is usually the note the chord is named after. Then decide whether the chord on top of it is major or minor, by playing both along with the recording: the wrong one is easy to hear. The key of the song narrows the choices to a handful. Below: each step in turn, what to do when a chord won't fit, and whether an app can do it for you.

## Why are chords hard to hear?

A chord is several notes at once, often spread over several instruments: the bass has one, the guitar a few, the piano a few more. Your ear doesn't hand them to you one by one. It hears a colour.

So don't ask "what chord is this?" all at once. Ask smaller questions, one at a time: where does the chord change, what's its lowest note, and is it major or minor? Three small answers add up to a chord.

The examples here are on guitar, but it works the same way on a piano, or on anything else that plays chords.

## 1. Pick a short passage and loop it

Not the whole song. Take one line of the verse, a few bars, and let it repeat. Most songs are built on a chord progression: a few chords in a row that come round again and again. So one line often gives you most of the song.

If you're starting out, pick a song where the chords are easy to hear: slow changes, a clear guitar or piano, and not much else going on. Here are [nine easy songs to transcribe](/blog/easy-songs-to-transcribe/), in order of difficulty, with no answers given away.

## 2. Hear where the chords change

Before you name anything, find the changes. Tap your foot, count the beats, and notice the moments when the ground shifts under the melody. Chords like to change at the start of a bar, and sometimes in the middle of one, so counting tells you where to listen. If the beat is hard to find, here's [how to find the BPM of a song](/blog/find-the-bpm-of-a-song-tap-tempo/), which is mostly about finding its beats and bars.

Now you know how many chords the passage has and how long each one lasts. That's your map. Shorten the loop to a single chord, so that only that chord plays.

## 3. Find the bass note

Listen for the lowest note under the chord and hum it. Then find the note you're humming on your instrument. On a guitar, slide a finger along the lowest string until the two match.

Most of the time, that note is the root: the note the chord is named after. If the bass plays a G, your first guess is some kind of G chord.

The bass can be hard to pick out, especially on a laptop or a phone. If it hides, here's [how to hear the bass in a song](/blog/hear-the-bass-in-a-song/): better headphones, an EQ, an octave up, or separating the bass from the rest of the band.

## 4. Decide: major or minor?

Play the major chord on that root along with the loop, then the minor one. One of them blends in and the other rubs. You don't need to know in advance which is which: your ear is much better at noticing a wrong chord than at naming a right one.

People say major sounds happy and minor sounds sad. It's a rough guide, and it stops helping when the chords go by quickly. Comparing with the recording always works.

If you want to practise this on its own, don't stop at saying "major" or "minor": sing the chord's notes from the bottom up. There's more on that in [is ear training worth it?](/blog/is-ear-training-worth-it/)

## 5. Let the key narrow the choices

After two or three chords you'll notice that the song keeps to a family. That family is the key. A song in a major key takes most of its chords from just six: three major and three minor. In G major, for instance, they're G, C and D, and A minor, B minor and E minor.

So once you know the key, the bass note nearly answers the whole question. An E in the bass, in a song in G? Try E minor first.

To find the key, look for the chord where the song sounds at rest, as if it could stop there. It's often the last chord of the song, and often the first.

Every chord you find makes the next one easier. But songs do step outside the family now and then. So use the key for guessing, and step 4 for knowing.

## 6. Play along, then move on

Play the progression you've found along with the passage, at full speed. If a bar sounds off, go back to that chord on its own. When the passage is right, move the loop on to the next one. A second verse is usually the first one again, so the work gets quicker as the song goes on.

## What if a chord still sounds wrong?

A few usual suspects:

- **The bass isn't playing the root.** Sometimes it plays another note of the chord, or walks from one chord to the next. Try the chords of the key that have the note you hear in them.
- **It's neither major nor minor.** With distorted guitars it's often a power chord: only the root and the note a fifth above it, with nothing in between to make it major or minor. The guitarist isn't telling, and you don't have to either: play just those two notes.
- **There's an extra note in it.** Sevenths, suspended chords and the like are a plain chord with a decoration. Get the plain chord first. If it sounds nearly right but a little bare, that's the moment to look for the extra note.
- **Everything is slightly off, whatever you play.** Then the recording may not be at your instrument's pitch. Bands tune down, and some old recordings are a little sharp or flat. Here's [how to tell that a song is tuned down](/blog/songs-tuned-half-a-step-down-eb-tuner/), and how to play along anyway.

## Do you need perfect pitch, or music theory?

No to both. You never have to name a note out of thin air: you compare what you play with what you hear, and matching two sounds is a skill that grows with use. A little theory does save time, and step 5 is all of it you need for now: which chords belong to a key.

## Is there an app that tells you the chords?

Yes. Chord detection apps, such as [Chordify](https://chordify.net), listen to a song and show you their guess. To be fair to them, they're quick, and handy for checking your work or when you need a chart in a hurry. But it's a guess, so you still need your ear to catch the mistakes, and reading the answers doesn't train it. I wrote more about that in [why transcribing by ear makes you a better musician](/blog/why-transcribe-music-by-ear/).
